# Chapter 6: Saying No Gracefully

At some point a colleague will ask you to do something with AI that you shouldn't do — paste client records into a free chatbot, let a recorder into a meeting, generate a document your policy prohibits. This chapter gives you words for those moments that are firm, warm, and blame-free.

## The three-part "no"

A good workplace "no" has three parts:

1. **Willingness.** You're glad to help with the underlying goal.
2. **The line.** Named briefly, without theatrics.
3. **An alternative.** A path forward that solves their actual problem.

> "Happy to help you get that summary done. I don't paste client records into public AI tools, though — that data shouldn't leave our systems. If you tell me what you're trying to find, I can do it from the clean copy and we'll have it in ten minutes."

Nobody loses face. The client data stays put. You're the helpful one.

## Phrases to keep in your pocket

- "I don't put anything with names on it into a public tool — but I can work from a de-identified version."
- "That document's marked internal, so it stays in our approved tools. Want me to summarize the gist instead?"
- "I'm not the right person to decide that; let's ask [IT / your manager / legal] before someone does it anyway."
- "Yes to the *goal*, no to the *method*. Here's the method I can do..."

Notice each one redirects to the actual need. That's usually all the other person wanted.

## Borrowing authority gracefully

If you have a policy to point at, use it — it depersonalizes the refusal. The line you found with the search from [Chapter 1](ch01-reading-employer-policy.md) becomes your receipt. Tested and working search from your own saved policy copies:

```console
$ grep -inE "confidential|internal use|client data" ~/company-docs/acceptable-use-policy.txt
4: Confidential client data may not be entered into unapproved tools.
9: "Internal use only" materials may not be shared outside the company.
```

Then your refusal gets shorter and even friendlier:

> "Quick heads-up — this one's covered by the acceptable-use policy, section on unapproved tools. Let me do the de-identified version instead."

You're not the one saying no. The policy is. You're just the courier of good news.

## When the asker is your manager

Same structure, more deference, same line. Managers navigate a hundred requests a day; a crisp flag that includes a compliant alternative is a *gift* to them:

> "I can absolutely get you that by Friday. One note: doing it this way would involve pasting client details into a tool our policy lists as unapproved. I'll start the de-identified version now and flag you if I hit a wall."

If they still insist? You've said the line once, clearly, in writing. Escalation has a chapter of its own — [Chapter 7](ch07-escalation-red-flags.html).

## The grace note

Sometimes the truth is that the request is *fine*, and your "no" is just discomfort. That's worth catching in yourself: not every AI use needs to be forbidden to be handled well. If the honest answer is "I'm just not confident doing it that fast," say the true thing — it invites help instead of confrontation.

## Checklist from this chapter

- Lead with willingness, state the line briefly, offer the alternative
- Point at the policy when you can — it takes you off the hook
- Never sarcastic, never lecture-y, never apocalyptic
- In writing (chat/email) when the stakes are real
